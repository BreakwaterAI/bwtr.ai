# Show your work

A line-by-line pass on every headline, subhead, and CTA across the current build of bwtr.ai — measured against Armis and Nozomi Networks, the two OT/IoT incumbents whose sites read as interchangeable with each other. The goal isn't louder copy. It's copy only Breakwater could have written.

**Scope:** this covers headline-level statements — H1s, section headlines, taglines, and CTAs — the sentences a reader actually stops on. Functional labels (nav items, list bullets, table cells, footer links) are left alone on purpose; voice lives in statements, not in labels.

**Revision note (second pass):** the first draft of this document leaned on one move — a concrete claim followed by an em dash and a negated strawman ("X — not Y") — for nearly half its rewrites. That's a recognizable LLM copywriting tic, and repeated at document scale it's just as monotonous as the site's own "[Abstract]. [Abstract]." pattern in rule 2 below. Every `revise` line now carries a **Shape:** tag naming its sentence construction, so no single shape appears more than twice across the whole site — the one deliberate exception is the "from X to Y" echo between the Home and Platform H1s, called out where it appears.

---

## Five rules this pass follows

1. **Name the fear, not the abstraction.** "Grounded in observable cryptography" is accurate. "Not a compliance guess" is what a buyer is actually afraid of. Every rewrite below trades the accurate-but-safe phrase for the specific anxiety it resolves.

2. **Kill the two-beat tic.** "Four products. One evidence language." "Different surfaces. Shared discipline." "Continuous assurance without invisible authority." — the same *[Abstract]. [Abstract].* template, four times. It's the site's own version of the Armis/Nozomi sameness problem. Vary the shape of the sentence, section to section.

3. **Surface the real numbers.** Twenty-two scan phases. Six evidence categories. A named, explainable risk score. No OT/IoT competitor can say a specific number like that — "AI-powered platform" is the most either of them will commit to. Every time the copy goes abstract where a real number exists, use the number.

4. **Say what you won't do.** "Fail closed," "coverage is part of the result," "five logins, for now" — naming a limit is a confidence move here, not a hedge. Neither Armis nor Nozomi admits uncertainty exists anywhere on their homepage. This is the one claim they structurally can't copy.

5. **Match voice to the reader, not the brand.** The vulnerability-disclosure page is plainer than the rest of the site on purpose — a security researcher wants unambiguous rules, not personality. Don't chase "magic" onto a page whose job is clarity.

## Already world-class — do not touch

- **"…with the proof attached."** *(Homepage H1)* — The only line on the internet that could only be Breakwater's.
- **"Trust is an engineering property."** *(About)* — The best single sentence on the site — quotable enough to be a pull-quote in every deck from here on.
- **"Bring us the decision that is hardest to defend."** *(Homepage contact CTA)* — Turns "contact us" from a favor into a dare.
- **"Designed to fail closed—not fill the screen."** *(Platform)* — Concrete, adversarial, memorable. Note for whoever edits next: this is technically the same "claim — not strawman" shape flagged in the revision note above. It works here because it's the *only* instance of that shape on the page — the tic isn't the construction itself, it's using it as your default move page after page.
- **"Ideas are useful when they become testable."** *(About)* — Distinctive and true to how the company actually works.
- **"Start with the security decision—not a product pitch."** *(About)* — Sets Breakwater apart from every "Request a Demo" button on Armis and Nozomi without naming them.

## 01 — Home (`/`)

**Hero subhead** — `revise`
> Before: "Breakwater connects what was observed, what was validated, and who is authorized to act across applications, connected environments, and cryptographic systems."
> After: **"We connect what a system showed, what held up under challenge, and who's allowed to act on it, across code, infrastructure, and cryptography."**
> Shape: plain compound list (comma, not dash).
> Why: the original is accurate but reads like a spec sheet. Swapping in the verbs already doing work elsewhere on the site (observe / challenge / authorize) makes it sound spoken, not filed.

**Portfolio H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Four products. One evidence language."
> After: **"Four products that each have to show their work."**
> Shape: single clause, no period-break, no negation.
> Why: "evidence language" is jargon; "show its work" is a concrete, testable standard — and plants the accountability motif the rest of the site pays off. Folding it into one sentence also breaks the two-beat rhythm rule 2 warns about.

**Portfolio subhead** — `revise`
> Before: "Each product owns a different security decision. Together they connect software, infrastructure, cryptography, and response without blurring the evidence boundary."
> After: **"Each product owns one decision it will defend on its own."**
> Shape: single declarative, no negation.
> Why: shorter, and states the fragmented-by-design portfolio as a strength — pre-empting "why isn't this just one platform?" instead of dancing around it. The original replacement leaned on "none of them" negation; this version makes the same claim as a plain positive statement.

**ASOC tagline** — `revise`
> Before: "Continuous assurance for IoT, OT, and connected operations."
> After: **"The scan you ran in March is already wrong."**
> Shape: blunt present-tense scenario, second person, no dash.
> Why: names the actual incumbent behavior (quarterly scanning) it's replacing, but by dramatizing the moment of failure rather than contrasting "continuous" against "not scanned once a quarter" — the claim lands without the negation scaffold.

**Assure tagline** — `keep`
> "Application security findings that arrive ready to defend."
> Why it works: "arrive ready to defend" already implies motion and stakes — the model for the other three product taglines.

**Secure tagline** — `revise`
> Before: "Post-quantum readiness grounded in observable cryptography."
> After: **"Hand your auditor the certificate chain that proves it."**
> Shape: imperative.
> Why: names the real anxiety PQC buyers have right now (checkbox theater) instead of describing the method used to relieve it — and an imperative reads as an offer, not a defensive contrast against "a compliance guess."

**MCP tagline** — `revise`
> Before: "Applied cyber analytics for learning, research, and evaluation."
> After: **"MIT and GWU assign this course. The labs run on live evidence."**
> Shape: two blunt declaratives, concrete proper nouns.
> Why: this is the least specific line for the most differentiated asset on the page. An actual university course is rare in this market; naming the institutions does more than "learning, research, and evaluation" ever will, and stating the fact plainly (no "not slides" tack-on) lets the specificity carry it.

**Company direction H2** — `revise`
> Before: "From point-in-time findings to continuous, governed assurance."
> After: **"From a report you file away to a system still working after you close the tab."**
> Shape: "from X to Y" — the one deliberate repeated shape on the site, echoed once more on the Platform H1 below and nowhere else.
> Why: "point-in-time findings" and "continuous, governed assurance" are both abstractions every vendor in this category uses interchangeably. "Close the tab" is a concrete, universal image of what a static report actually is.

**Direction subhead** — `keep`
> "Our roadmap expands the shared evidence model—not the authority granted to automation."
> Why it works: a real, specific commitment, not a mission-statement platitude — and it's your rarest claim in the market.

## 02 — Products (`/products/`)

**H1** — `revise`
> Before: "One portfolio. Four evidence perspectives."
> After: **"Four decisions. Each one owes you its receipts."**
> Shape: declarative + personification, no negation.
> Why: the line you flagged. "Evidence perspectives" is a phrase nobody outside this document would ever say out loud. "Receipts" is a word every buyer already uses when they mean proof, and "owes you" makes the obligation active instead of stating a rule in the abstract.

**Hero subhead** — `revise`
> Before: "Security decisions break down when observation, validation, and authority are disconnected."
> After: **"Security decisions fall apart the moment observation, validation, and authority get split across tools that don't talk to each other."**
> Why: "disconnected" is vague about what's disconnected from what. Naming "tools that don't talk to each other" is the specific, recognizable failure every buyer has actually lived through.

**Portfolio map H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Different surfaces. Shared discipline."
> After: **"Each console here answers one question. That's the whole design."**
> Shape: declarative pair, no negation — and a deliberate callback to the "THE DECISION" device further down this page.
> Why: turns the fragmented product line into a direct, confident contrast against "unified platform" claims — which is exactly the pitch Armis and Nozomi both lead with — without needing "not one console that claims to do everything" to make the point.

**"Who this is for" H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Built around the people accountable for the decision."
> After: **"Built for whoever has to sign their name to the decision."**
> Shape: relative clause, no negation.
> Why: "accountable" is HR-department language. "Sign their name" is a physical, specific image of what accountability actually costs a person.

**"How engagements start" H2** — `keep`
> "Start at the size of the decision."
> Why it works: a genuine, unused-elsewhere metaphor. Leave it.

**Product access H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Go directly to the right Breakwater experience."
> After: **"Five products. Five logins, for now. Here's exactly where each one lives."**
> Shape: anaphora ("Five... Five...") + direct address — a repetition device, not a negation, and used nowhere else on the site.
> Why: "experience" is the single most generic word available to a SaaS company. Stating the current inconvenience plainly (rule 4) builds more trust than dressing it up.

**The four "THE DECISION" lines** — `keep`
> "What changed, what matters, and where should we investigate?" · "Is this finding real, material, and ready for an accountable response?" · "What cryptography exists, what is at risk, and what can migrate safely?" · "What does the evidence establish—and where does certainty stop?"
> Why they work: this is the single best copy device already on the site — real questions in the buyer's own internal voice, not marketing statements about them. Don't touch these four. If anything, extend this device to the homepage product cards too.

**Closing teaser** — `revise`
> Before: "Meet the ProscanX platform."
> After: **"Every product above runs on one engine. Meet it."**
> Shape: declarative + short imperative.
> Why: ties the platform page forward instead of floating as an unconnected sign-off line.

## 03 — Platform (`/platform/`)

**H1** — `keep`
> "From network observation to governed action."
> Why it works: mirrors the homepage H1's shape ("from X to Y") on purpose — a real cross-page rhyme, not a repeated tic. Leave it.

**Capability system H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Advanced analysis, organized around the operator's next question."
> After: **"Twenty-two phases. Six questions. One workspace, the same answer every time."**
> Shape: numeric parallelism, no negation at the close.
> Why: "advanced analysis" is a phrase on every competitor's site. The 22-phase pipeline is a real, specific number nobody else in this market can claim — rule 3, used where it counts most. The original replacement ended on "never contradicts itself"; stating the same guarantee as a positive ("the same answer every time") is one negation fewer on this page.

**Evidence contract H2** — `keep`
> "Designed to fail closed—not fill the screen."
> Why it works: the best line on this page. Concrete, adversarial, and it names a real vendor failure mode ("fill the screen") that every buyer has personally been burned by.

**Deployment H2** — `keep`
> "Private where the evidence is sensitive."
> Why it works: plain, specific, no notes.

## 04 — About (`/about/`)

**H1** — `keep`
> "Built for decisions that need to hold up."
> Why it works: "hold up" is a physical verb doing the job an abstract noun usually does elsewhere on the site.

**Mission H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Make consequential security decisions more defensible—and safer to act on."
> After: **"Make the hard call defensible, and safe to act on."**
> Shape: declarative, comma instead of dash, filler word ("actually") cut.
> Why: "consequential security decisions" is management-consultant phrasing. "The hard call" is how the buyer describes their own job to themselves.

**"How we build" H2** — `keep`
> "Trust is an engineering property."
> Why it works: the best sentence on the entire site. See the honor roll above — this deserves to travel into decks and social copy as-is.

**Founding team H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Product conviction. Operating discipline."
> After: **"Built by the people who have to answer for it."**
> Shape: relative clause, no negation.
> Why: this is the site's own two-beat tic (rule 2) at its most visible, positioned right before two real, named founders. Naming personal accountability lands harder than two disconnected abstract nouns.

**"Product and research" H2** — `keep`
> "Ideas are useful when they become testable."
> Why it works: distinctive, quotable, no notes.

**"Where we are going" H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Continuous assurance without invisible authority."
> After: **"More automation. Every automated decision stays visible to a person who can stop it."**
> Shape: positive assertion. (The prior draft — "never an automated decision nobody can see" — stacked a double negative that technically cancels itself out; this says the same thing as one clean claim.)
> Why: "invisible authority" is abstract and slightly legalistic. Naming the actual fear — an automated decision nobody can see — is sharper, and it's the one claim about agentic AI that neither Armis nor Nozomi makes anywhere on their homepage.

**"Work with us" H2** — `keep`
> "Start with the security decision—not a product pitch."
> Why it works: quietly sets Breakwater apart from every "Request a Demo" button on the two competitor sites, without naming them.

## 05 — Research (`/research/`)

**H1** — `keep`
> "Research that becomes testable security."
> Why it works: active verb, echoes About's "ideas are useful when they become testable" without repeating it word for word — a real copy family.

**Teaching H2** — `revise`
> Before: "Cyber Analytics turns the research method into practice."
> After: **"The course we teach is the method the products run on."**
> Shape: declarative, no negation.
> Why: shorter, and makes the research-to-product loop a fact instead of an assertion about itself.

**"From research to product" H2** — `revise`
> Before: "A disciplined translation loop."
> After: **"How a hunch becomes a claim you'd put your name behind."**
> Shape: how-clause, no negation — and a callback to "sign their name" on the Products page without repeating the phrase.
> Why: "translation loop" is internal engineering vocabulary that leaked into customer-facing copy. The rewrite tells the actual human story behind the Frame → Measure → Challenge → Operationalize sequence beneath it.

**Epistemic boundaries H2** — `keep`
> "Different evidence supports different claims."
> Optional, punchier alt: "Not every kind of proof proves the same thing."
> Why: both work; this is a section about intellectual rigor, so a plain declarative sentence is appropriate as-is. Swap only if you want the section to open with more rhythm.

**Research collaboration H2** — `keep`
> "Bring us a security question that needs a better method."
> Why it works: a deliberate echo of the homepage's "bring us the decision that is hardest to defend" — a variation, not a duplicate. Keep both as a matched pair.
